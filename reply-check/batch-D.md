### D1. The "gantlet": six links, 82% each, 50% each
- **Pinker:** "For a chain of six links to yield Alexander's 30 percent estimate, each link (superintelligent AI, murderous goal, domination of infrastructure, etc.) would have to have a probability of about 82 percent. Conversely, if there were only an even chance at each step (still generous), the product falls below two percent. Make a few of the steps long shots, and it becomes negligible." (L58)
- **Verdict:** 🟡 The arithmetic is right. Whether multiplying six stages is a fair method is exactly what is disputed, and Pinker's version has the features the critique targets.
- **Evidence (arithmetic) `[MEASURED, my pull]`:**
  - 0.82^6 = 0.3040; 0.3^(1/6) = 0.8182 (so "about 82 percent" is right); 0.5^6 = 0.015625 = 1.56% ("below two percent" is right).
  - Scott's number is 20% in his own post ("My AI Opinions") and "25-30%" in the open letter, so "Alexander's 30 percent" is the top of his range (other batch, A2).
- **Evidence (the multiple-stage-fallacy critique):**
  - Yudkowsky, "The Multiple Stage Fallacy" (Facebook, 2016-03, reposted on the EA Forum): "The Multiple-Stage Fallacy is when you list multiple 'stages' that need to happen on the way to some final outcome, assign probabilities to each 'stage', multiply the probabilities together, and end up with a small final answer." ([EA Forum copy](https://ea.greaterwrong.com/posts/GgPrbxdWhyaDjks2m/the-multiple-stage-fallacy)) `[V]`
  - His three errors, verbatim: "1. First and foremost, you need to multiply *conditional* probabilities rather than the absolute probabilities." "2. Often, people neglect to consider disjunctive alternatives—there may be more than one way to reach a stage, so that not *all* the listed things need to happen." "3. People have tendencies to assign middle-tending probabilities. So if you list enough stages, you can drive the apparent probability of anything down to zero, even if you solicit probabilities from the reader." Also "3a. If you're a motivated skeptic, you will be tempted to list more 'stages'." `[V]`
  - His example is Nate Silver's six 50% "stages of doom" for Trump: 0.5^6 = 1.6%, the same arithmetic as Pinker's "even chance at each step". Markets later put Trump at 74%. `[V]`
  - Follow-up on the arithmetic: Pinker's six links are stated as bare prose with no conditionals. Stage 2 ("develop the goal of murdering every last man, woman, and child") is not independent of stage 1, and "execute its plan flawlessly in a world governed by Murphy's Law" reads as an extra stage added on top. `[my reading]`
- **Evidence (defences of staged multiplication, and why the critique is not a refutation):**
  - Jeff Kaufman's reply to Yudkowsky: "I agree [that you must use conditional probabilities], and that's what I did", and "I think this approach is probably a good one to use in general", while conceding it is "a reason to take predictions you make this way less seriously" ([Kaufman 2016-03-17](https://www.jefftk.com/p/multiple-stage-fallacy#:~:text=I%20think%20this%20approach,one%20to%20use)) `[V]`
  - Joe Carlsmith multiplies six premises (65%, 80%, 40%, 65%, 40%, 95%) to get about 5% for existential catastrophe by 2070, then says the estimate "has gone up, and is now at >10%" ([arXiv abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.13353#:~:text=my%20estimate%20here%20has,now%20at%20%3E10%25)) `[V]` for the quoted update; the six premise figures are `[2nd]` (search summary). So staged multiplication is also the doomers' own tool, and it can land above Pinker's 2%.
  - The same trap works in reverse: "Disjunctive arguments can be a reverse multiple-stage fallacy" (LessWrong linkpost, 2026-01-31) ([LW](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/BMX4pyPLFRLr8BY9D/disjunctive-arguments-can-be-a-reverse-multiple-stage)) `[V]`
  - Mirror-image chain in the FRI adversarial collaboration: a participant says skeptics reason "for humans to go extinct, events A, B, C, and D need to happen ... so P(extinction) = P(A) P(B) P(C) P(D) = [low number]. Call this style of reasoning *default-success*" and the concerned reason "for humans to *not* go extinct, events X, Y, Z need to happen; thus P(success) = ... [relatively low number]. Call this style of reasoning *default-failure*" (Teshi, [FRI "Roots of Disagreement", PDF p. 72-73](https://forecastingresearch.org/pdf/roots-of-disagreement-on-ai-risk.pdf#page=72)) `[V]`. In that report both camps run a conjunction; the dispute is which event is the chain.
  - Rinehart (AEI, D6) describes the same split in the Yudkowsky-Patel interview: Patel "treats doom as the end of a long chain of events", Yudkowsky "treats *survival* as the fragile chain" `[2nd]`.
- **Context / what's left out:**
  - The fair statement is: a product of six bare numbers is only as good as the conditionals and the exhaustiveness of the path list. It is a method for exposing what you would have to believe (82% per link), not evidence that the answer is low. Pinker's line "for 30 percent each link would need 82 percent" is a legitimate reductio only if the links are roughly independent and there is one path; with positive correlation (a capable, goal-directed, deceptive system makes later stages likelier) the 30% needs far less than 82% on the later conditionals. Worked example `[MEASURED, my pull]`: conditionals 0.9, 0.9, 0.8, 0.7, 0.6, 0.9 multiply to 24%.
  - Disjunction: RAND (D3) itself finds several routes (pathogens, geoengineering, nuclear) and says it could not rule out any; two independent 2% routes give 3.96%, five independent 10% routes give 41% `[MEASURED, my pull]`. Pinker's chain has one route.
  - Whether Pinker's step 3 ("conceal that goal ... and escape their control") is a necessary stage or one of several is the substantive disagreement; the arithmetic cannot settle it.
- **How checked:** python arithmetic; read Yudkowsky's post, Kaufman's reply, the LW disjunctive post and FRI's 151-page "Roots of Disagreement" PDF text; Carlsmith's abstract from arXiv.

### D2. FutureSearch and the one percent
- **Pinker:** "When I put the question to FutureSearch, which Alexander calls 'the AI superforecaster' ('Will AI cause the extinction of the human race (all or nearly all humans killed as a result of AI actions) by December 31, 2031?'), it proffered the smallest round number, one percent, noting that the scenario 'requires compressing an unprecedented series of cascading failures into a very tight five-year window.'" (L58); "Recall as well that the AI system that Alexander himself calls 'the AI superforecaster,' FutureSearch, estimated the chance of AI doom by the end of 2031 as one percent." (L78)
- **Verdict:** 🟡 FutureSearch is a real AI forecasting company Scott has written about repeatedly, but Scott does not call it "the AI superforecaster"; and Scott's own recent posts warn against exactly this use. Pinker's query result is unverifiable `[❔]`.
- **Evidence:**
  - Scott, 2026-07-02: "FutureSearch - the company that claims to be beating the stock market - kindly offered to let me try their AI superforecaster and write about it here." ([ACX](https://www.astralcodexten.com/p/the-ai-superforecasters-are-here#:~:text=FutureSearch%20%2D%20the%20company,their%20AI%20superforecaster)) `[V]` ("their", and "an AI superforecaster" is a category: "An AI superforecaster is an AI ... modified to be good at forecasting.")
  - Same post: "The only AI superforecaster that I know of which is currently open for public use is FutureSearch" ([ACX](https://www.astralcodexten.com/p/the-ai-superforecasters-are-here#:~:text=The%20only%20AI%20superforecaster,use%20is%20FutureSearch)) `[V]`. He also names Preseen as a second AI superforecaster, and the 2026-11-19 post lists "FutureSearch, Mantic, Preseen". So "the" is Pinker's definite article; Scott's is "an"/"their" plus "the only ... open for public use".
  - FutureSearch is "the company that claims to be beating the stock market" (Scott, above); Scott says "Claims by the AI startups themselves may be treated skeptically" `[V]` (same post, sources/D-acx-ai-superforecasters-are-here.txt).
  - Scott's own caveats on this very use:
    - 2026-07-02: "We have less data about how they deal with long-term, society-wide, and truly weird events." ([ACX](https://www.astralcodexten.com/p/the-ai-superforecasters-are-here#:~:text=We%20have%20less%20data,truly%20weird%20events)) `[V]`
    - Same post, when FutureSearch gave 1% for a US-China AI-slowdown treaty: "I just figured it was outside the distribution of things that AI superforecasters are probably good at. This isn't too crazy - in the past, 'AI experts', including many rationalists and safety advocates, have outperformed superforecasters at predicting the future course of AI." `[V]`
    - 2026-11-19, four days before Pinker's essay: "And I know that AIs are bad at forecasting the speed of AI progress or anything downstream of it" ([ACX](https://www.astralcodexten.com/p/the-ai-forecasting-agenda#:~:text=And%20I%20know%20that,downstream%20of%20it)) `[V]`
  - Pinker's own query: no link, no transcript, no date. FutureSearch's app is not publicly archived for this query. `[❔]` I could not reproduce it (running the query would use a paid account).
- **Context / what's left out:**
  - The question Pinker asked FutureSearch is dated (by 2031-12-31) and phrased "all or nearly all humans killed", not extinction. Scott's 20-30% is undated (see D10), and Scott says "p(doom) calculations don't give points for delay - maybe centuries later" ([ACX "My AI Opinions"](https://www.astralcodexten.com/p/my-ai-opinions)). A 1% chance by 2031 is compatible with a 20-30% eventual chance; "Recall as well" compares two different quantities (L78). Likewise the XPT numbers in D8 are "by 2100".
  - The quoted rationale ("a very tight five-year window") is the model's own words about the 2031 deadline, so it is a statement about timelines rather than about the final probability.
- **How checked:** fetched both ACX posts; WebSearch to find them; read post.md for any FutureSearch mention (none); no scry needed.

### D3. RAND RR-A3034-1
- **Pinker:** "the authors of the RAND Corporation's [2025 analysis of AI extinction risk], which granted the (tendentious, to put it mildly) assumption that AI had the objective of wiping out humanity, wrote that though they 'cannot categorically prove the negative; that is, we cannot assert the impossibility of such an event occurring': > the capabilities and concerted efforts required to pose an extinction threat to human beings [e.g., secretly releasing megatons of a super-greenhouse gas] are immense, primarily because of the inherent adaptability and resilience of humans. Given the opportunity, it is expected that humans would actively respond and effectively implement measures to mitigate any such threats and survive the aftermath." (L58-60)
- **Verdict:** 🟡 Both quotations are verbatim (the first-letter misquote is gone), but they are selected from a report that says the opposite of "negligible", and the bracket points at the one scenario where RAND says humans may not be able to respond.
- **Evidence (verbatim):**
  - "The capabilities and concerted efforts required to pose an extinction threat to human beings are immense, primarily because of the inherent adaptability and resilience of humans. Given the opportunity, it is expected that humans would actively respond and effectively implement measures to mitigate any such threats and survive the aftermath." (RAND p. 40, [PDF p. 52](https://www.rand.org/content/dam/rand/pubs/research_reports/RRA3000/RRA3034-1/RAND_RRA3034-1.pdf#page=52); [local](../docs/RAND_RRA3034-1.html#:~:text=The%20capabilities%20and%20concerted,to%20human%20beings%20are)) `[V]`
  - "Although we outline the substantial challenges and likely human responses that would counteract such threats, we cannot categorically prove the negative; that is, we cannot assert the impossibility of such an event occurring. Each scenario, regardless of the challenges, still holds a potential risk of becoming an extinction threat depending on how complex adaptive systems will respond in the event of a catastrophe." (p. 40; [local](../docs/RAND_RRA3034-1.html#:~:text=we%20cannot%20categorically%20prove%20the%20negative)) `[V]`
  - Order: in RAND the "adaptability" passage comes first and the "cannot categorically prove the negative" sentence follows in the next paragraph; Pinker reverses them (harmless).
  - The bracket "[e.g., secretly releasing megatons of a super-greenhouse gas]" is Pinker's insertion, not in RAND's sentence `[V]` (compare the text above). The geoengineering scenario is the one RAND singles out as different: "In only one of our scenarios-malicious geoengineering-humans might not be able to respond to and bounce back from a threat. This is because the effects of such a scenario would persist for hundreds or thousands of years with no known mitigation, and an extinction-level tipping point might be reached before any catastrophic effects of malicious geoengineering are even felt." (p. 40-41; [local](../docs/RAND_RRA3034-1.html#:~:text=In%20only%20one%20of%20our%20scenarios)) `[V]` So the example Pinker chose for "humans would actively respond ... and survive the aftermath" is the scenario RAND says humans might not be able to respond to.
  - Pinker's "omits the falsification statement": RAND's summary: "We found that this scenario does present a true extinction threat and a potential falsification of our hypothesis" (geoengineering) and of pathogens: "We were not able to determine whether this scenario presents a likely extinction risk for humanity, but we cannot rule out the possibility. This scenario represents a true extinction threat and a potential falsification of our hypothesis." (p. v-vi; [local](../docs/RAND_RRA3034-1.html#:~:text=This%20scenario%20represents%20a%20true%20extinction)) `[V]`
  - Conclusion: "We found that two of our scenarios-those involving biological threats and malicious geoengineering-presented a potential falsification of our hypothesis that AI is not capable of conclusively causing the extinction of humanity ... even in the third scenario (nuclear weapons), although we could not show that AI definitely could create an extinction threat to humanity, we could not rule out the possibility. Ultimately, we do not definitively assert whether any of the three scenarios we explored are likely or unlikely to be extinction threats to humanity. These scenarios are meant to be exploratory, not predictive." (RAND p. 49, [PDF p. 61](https://www.rand.org/content/dam/rand/pubs/research_reports/RRA3000/RRA3034-1/RAND_RRA3034-1.pdf#page=61)) `[V]`
  - Key finding headline: "Extinction threats are immensely challenging but cannot be ruled out. ... Nevertheless, we could not rule out the possibility in any of our scenarios" (p. vi; [local](../docs/RAND_RRA3034-1.html#:~:text=we%20could%20not%20rule,any%20of%20our%20scenarios)) `[V]`
  - RAND refuses to give probabilities: "We assert that predictions about the likelihood of extinction risks from AI are inappropriate as analytical tools given the deep uncertainties that preclude useful, policy-relevant predictions." (p. vi; [local](../docs/RAND_RRA3034-1.html#:~:text=predictions%20about%20the%20likelihood,inappropriate%20as%20analytical%20tools)) `[V]` That supports Pinker's scepticism about p(doom) numbers (D10) but also means RAND cannot be cited for his "negligible" product; it also contradicts "tendentious": RAND says "We assert that the potential benefits of AI make the shut-it-down approach to AI governance inappropriate" (p. vi), i.e. it is not a doomer document. `[V]`
- **"Granted the assumption that AI had the objective of wiping out humanity":**
  - Partly right. RAND: "human extinction would not be a plausible outcome unless an actor was intentionally seeking that outcome" (p. v; [local](../docs/RAND_RRA3034-1.html#:~:text=human%20extinction%20would%20not,an%20actor%20was%20intentionally)) and "such an event ... could not happen merely by accident; it would require a threat actor to actively pursue the goal of human extinction" (p. 39-40) `[V]`.
  - But RAND's actor is generic: "We conceive of AI as a threat actor ... The threat actor might or might not have the intent to harm or even have agency." and "We do not assume that AI would have agency (which we define in this report as the ability to set one's own objectives)" (p. 3; [local](../docs/RAND_RRA3034-1.html#:~:text=We%20conceive%20of%20AI%20as%20a%20threat)) `[V]`; and "we assume that the AI system has a correlated objective to survive long enough to achieve its primary goal" ([local](../docs/RAND_RRA3034-1.html#:~:text=we%20assume%20that%20the,to%20survive%20long%20enough)) `[V]`. Also "it remains unclear how AI, specifically, would take those actions" (p. 49) `[V]`.
  - So "granted the assumption that AI had the objective" is a fair compression of the scenario design; "tendentious, to put it mildly" is Pinker's editorial. RAND's own stance is that the intent assumption is a modelling device, so that the analysis can ask what capabilities extinction would need.
- **Comparison with the first letter (`../report.md` section 2):**
  - First letter: RAND "concluded that there is 'no describable scenario in which AI is conclusively an extinction threat to humanity'". That sentence is RAND's starting hypothesis (p. v), not its finding; Scott said so, and the earlier report rated Scott substantially right.
  - The reply drops that sentence and quotes the real conclusion passage instead. The description "granted the assumption that AI had the objective of wiping out humanity" is repeated from the first letter nearly word for word. The reply never says it corrected the misquote, and it does not mention Scott's RAND rebuttal (RAND appears once in the essay, at L58).
  - What it still omits: the two "potential falsification" findings and "could not rule out" in all three scenarios. The bracketed geoengineering example, as above.
- **How checked:** fetched the RAND PDF (`../RAND_RRA3034-1.pdf`, pdftotext, page numbers cross-checked against printed numbers: PDF p. 52 = printed 40, PDF p. 5 = printed v, PDF p. 15 = printed 3) and compared with the local HTML; reused `../report.md` section 2; Nordhaus & Trembath (D9) also describe RAND as having "attempted to falsify potential extinction scenarios" and call the geoengineering route "hardly more credible than paperclip maximizing" `[V]` ([Breakthrough Journal](https://www.breakthroughjournal.org/p/pdoom-is-stupid)), another reading Pinker doesn't mention.

### D4. Caplan-Yudkowsky bet
- **Pinker:** "(It can, however, be bet upon. In 2017, the doom sceptic Bryan Caplan advanced US$100 to Yudkowsky, who gets to enjoy the money in the world's remaining years, but must repay Caplan US$200 in inflation-adjusted dollars if the world hasn't ended by 1 January 2030.)" (L70)
- **Verdict:** ✅ Terms are right. One omission: the bet is about the world ending "by nonaligned AI", with definitions.
- **Evidence:**
  - "Bryan Caplan pays Eliezer $100 now, in exchange for $200 CPI-adjusted from Eliezer if the world has not been ended by nonaligned AI before 12:00am GMT on January 1st, 2030." ([Caplan, EconLog 2017-01-17, archived copy](https://web.archive.org/web/20261022222039/https://www.econlib.org/archives/2017/01/my_end-of-the-w.html#:~:text=CPI%2Dadjusted%20from%20Eliezer%20if,by%20nonaligned%20AI)) `[V]` (live page returns 403 to curl; the Wayback copy 2026-10-22 has the full text.)
  - "If there are still biological humans running around on the surface of the Earth, it will not have been said to be ended." and "$100 USD is due to Eliezer Yudkowsky before February 1st, 2017 for the bet to become effective." `[V]`
  - Caplan's own gloss: "In my mind, this is primarily a bet that annualized real interest rates stay below 5.5%. After all, at 5.5%, I could turn $100 today in $200 inflation-adjusted without betting." (same post; [link](https://web.archive.org/web/20261022222039/https://www.econlib.org/archives/2017/01/my_end-of-the-w.html#:~:text=annualized%20real%20interest%20rates%20stay%20below%205.5%25)) `[V]` And: "The bet is structured so that Eliezer still gets a marginal benefit ($100 now) even if he's right about the end of the world." `[V]` So the bet is not a clean read of either man's probability.
  - Follow-ups (not needed for the check) `[2nd]`: Caplan posted "NINTH year ... I WILL win on January 1, 2030" in March 2026 and opened a second bet with Greg Colburn on the same terms in April 2026 (search summaries only; sources not fetched).
- **Context / what's left out:**
  - This bet is a counter-example to Pinker's own D10 claim that p(doom) "has no deadline" and "can never be scored": it has a deadline, and it resolves in the no-doom branch. Pinker concedes this in the parenthesis ("It can, however, be bet upon").
  - "Doom sceptic" is fair (Caplan: "I find this worry fanciful"). "advanced" is accurate; the money was paid in 2017.
- **How checked:** fetched the EconLog page via the archive (raw HTML, extracted text in sources/D-caplan-econlib-2017.txt).

### D5. Tetlock et al. 2025, Risk Analysis
- **Pinker:** "As Tetlock and his collaborators put it in 2025, 'There is no evidence their skills generalize beyond 12-24 months into the future, a temporal zone where cautious base-rate-grounded forecasting often works well and bold claims about abrupt discontinuities fare poorly.'" (L72, doi 10.1111/risa.70023). L239: "The sceptical forecasters in Tetlock's adversarial collaboration cited exactly this record: the 'previous predictions of technology-driven, mega-disasters that either proved wrong or have yet to be realized.'"
- **Verdict:** ❔ I could not obtain the full text, so neither quotation is verified. What I could verify points against reading the first sentence as the authors' own view.
- **What the paper is:** Rosenberg, Karger, Jacobs, Hickman, Morris, Durland, Kuusela and Tetlock, "Belief updating in AI-risk debates: Exploring the limits of adversarial collaboration", Risk Analysis 45(12), 2025 (published 2025-04-03; PMID 40180607). Abstract `[V]` (Europe PMC API and the [FRI page](https://forecastingresearch.org/research/belief-updating-in-ai-risk-debates-exploring-the-limits-of-adversarial-collaboration)): "We organized adversarial collaborations between subject-matter experts and expert forecasters with opposing views on whether recent advances in Artificial Intelligence (AI) pose an existential threat to humanity in the 21st century. Two studies incentivized participants to engage in respectful perspective-taking ... AI experts saw greater threats from AI than did expert forecasters, and neither group changed its long-term risk estimates, but they did preregister cruxes whose resolution by 2030 would sway their views on long-term risk. These persistent differences shrank as questioning moved across centuries, from 2100 to 2500 and beyond, by which time both groups put the risk of extreme negative outcomes from AI at 30%-40%."
- **Full-text attempts (all failed):**
  - Wiley full text and PDF: Cloudflare 403 (curl and WebFetch); r.jina.ai returned the Cloudflare page.
  - OpenAlex `W4409179170`: `is_oa: false`, no repository copy. Unpaywall: `oa_status: closed`, no OA locations. Semantic Scholar: `openAccessPdf.status: CLOSED`. Europe PMC: not in PMC (`isOpenAccess: N`). Crossref: license vor (Wiley TDM only). PubMed record has the abstract only.
  - ResearchGate: 403. Wayback: 404 for the Wiley URL. archive.today: unreachable. The FRI page only links to Wiley. A WebSearch summary said the paper is "open access in Risk Analysis"; the OpenAlex and Unpaywall records say it is not.
  - The two sentences Pinker quotes do not appear in the FRI predecessor working paper (the 151-page "Roots of Disagreement on AI Risk", March 2024: no hit for "12-24", "abrupt discontinuities", "mega-disaster"), nor in the arXiv paper "Why They Disagree" (2512.06350).
- **Whose view? What I can say:**
  - In the predecessor, the authors' own statement on horizons is the opposite in spirit: "Tetlock et al. found that, at least on some types of questions, experts are more accurate than simple base rate extrapolation over 25 year horizons, although they are much less accurate than they were over 0-2 years. ([FRI Roots, PDF p. 16 note 22](https://forecastingresearch.org/pdf/roots-of-disagreement-on-ai-risk.pdf#page=16)) `[V]` Scott's letter cites the same 25-year study (Tetlock et al. 2023, "Long-Range Subjective Probability Forecasts of Slow-Motion Variables in World Politics") as showing skilled forecasters "could still do relatively well predicting events as far out as 25 years" (post.md). The 2023 abstract: "Analyses of 5, 10 and 25-year cumulative-risk judgments made in 1988 and 1997 revealed: (a) specialists beat generalists at predicting nuclear proliferation but not shifting nation-state boundaries ... (c) accuracy fell faster on topics where expertise conferred no edge in shorter-range forecasts." ([local](../docs/Long-range%20subjective%20probability%20-%20Tetlock%20et%20al%202023%20%28accepted%20manuscript%29.html#:~:text=Analyses%20of%205%2C%2010%20and%2025%2Dyear%20cumulative%2Drisk%20judgments)) `[V]` So "no evidence ... beyond 12-24 months" is not what Tetlock's own long-range paper says; if the 2025 sentence is the authors', it is at best a statement about the AI questions.

If both come from the sceptics' reasoning, L72 misattributes. I could not confirm either way.
  - What the skeptics said in the predecessor (`[V]`, PDF p. 20 and 72): "Risk-concerned team spends too much time in silos that lack ideological diversity, gaming out doom-loop scenarios based on theories that will likely have little bearing on reality. (See: Y2K)" (Blake) and "A story demonstrating how a catastrophe could happen is not a good basis for a probabilistic forecast" (Pascal). That supports the L239 description (skeptics cite failed technology-doom predictions) in spirit, with Y2K as the example. I did not find the exact phrase "previous predictions of technology-driven, mega-disasters" there.
  - Omitted from the paper's abstract, which Pinker does not mention: "by 2500 and beyond, both groups put the risk of extreme negative outcomes from AI at 30%-40%." The predecessor says the skeptics gave 30% and the concerned group 40% to "severe negative effects on humanity" over the next thousand years (PDF p. 2) `[V]`.

### D6. AEI "Don't just tell me your p(doom), tell me your conditionals"
- **Pinker:** "Finally, most p(doom)s are not conditioned on what people will do. An estimate of human extinction must somehow divine not only the probability of what a hypothetical misaligned AI might do, but the probabilities of all the things that any government, company, engineer, and citizen might do to stop it over the coming decades, and their probabilities of success." ### D8. XPT: 0.38% and 3%, "skin in the game"
- **Pinker:** "In Tetlock's Existential Risk Persuasion Tournament, held in 2022, the median superforecaster put the probability of AI-caused human extinction by 2100 at 0.38 percent. Even the median AI expert, once they had skin in the game, put it at three percent, a tenth of Alexander's own estimate." (L78)
- **Verdict:** 🟡 The two numbers are right. "Once they had skin in the game" is wrong as a description of the XPT's incentives, and "a tenth of Alexander's own estimate" compares a by-2100 number with an undated one.
- **Evidence (numbers) `[V]`:**
  - "The median superforecaster gives a 0.38% chance of AI-driven extinction by the end of the century, compared to 3% for the median domain expert." ([XPT report, PDF p. 27](https://forecastingresearch.org/pdf/existential-risk-persuasion-tournament.pdf#page=27)). Table 9: superforecasters 0.38% [0.10, 0.75]; domain experts 3% [0.49, 10.00]; non-domain experts 2% [1.00, 4.03].
  - These "domain experts" are the AI experts (n=29 for this question; the group table labels them "AI domain experts", [PDF p. 22](https://forecastingresearch.org/pdf/existential-risk-persuasion-tournament.pdf#page=22)). Same table: the AI experts' **arithmetic mean is 13.61%**, against the median 3%; superforecasters' mean 1.98% `[V]`. Pinker picks the median (fine, but the experts' distribution is much heavier than the median suggests).
  - Tournament held 2022 ✅ (Figure 9 runs Aug-Nov 2022).
  - Pinker's link `forecastingresearch.org/s/XPT.pdf` returns an HTML landing page ("Forecasting Existential Risks: Evidence from a Long-Run Forecasting Tournament"), not the PDF. The PDF is at `/pdf/existential-risk-persuasion-tournament.pdf` (754 pages).
- **Evidence (incentives) `[V]`:**
  - "When we report probabilities of long-run catastrophic and existential risk in this report, we report forecasters' own (unincentivized) beliefs. But, we rely on the incentivized forecasts to calculate measures of intersubjective accuracy." ([XPT PDF p. 12](https://forecastingresearch.org/pdf/existential-risk-persuasion-tournament.pdf#page=12)) So the headline 0.38% and 3% are explicitly unincentivized beliefs.
  - "for the longer-run questions, participants learned that they would be scored based on the accuracy of their reciprocal forecasts: the better they predicted what experts and superforecasters would predict for each question, the better their score." (PDF p. 12) and "For unresolvable questions (like the chance of human extinction) and for long-run questions ... forecasters were asked to give their own prediction but were also asked for (and incentivized based on) two additional forecasts: one predicting the median of the superforecasters' forecasts, and the other predicting the median of the experts' forecasts." ([PDF p. 87 (appendix)](https://forecastingresearch.org/pdf/existential-risk-persuasion-tournament.pdf#page=87)).
  - Other incentives: "ten $1,000 prizes for the most persuasive individual forecast rationales" (PDF p. 12), "The median participant who completed the tournament earned $2,500 in incentives" (PDF p. 14); questions resolving by 2030 were scored "using traditional forecasting metrics" (PDF p. 12).
  - So no one was paid for being right about extinction, and no one's extinction forecast could be scored against the world. Narayanan and Kapoor (D9) make the same point: "recall that in the case of the XPT, the incentive is actually to predict each others' forecasts to get around the problem of long time horizons" ([N&K](https://www.normaltech.ai/p/ai-existential-risk-probabilities)) `[V]`.
  - "Once ... [they] had skin in the game" also implies the numbers fell under incentives. The report finds the opposite: "Few minds were changed during the XPT, even among the most active participants, despite monetary incentives for persuading others." (PDF p. 5; Figure 9, PDF p. 39) `[V]`
- **Context / what's left out:**
  - The same XPT postmortem: superforecasters' 0.38% rose to 1% "conditional on AGI coming into existence by 2070" (PDF p. 28) `[V]`; the AI-caused catastrophe (10% of humanity dead) figure is 2.13% for superforecasters and 12% for AI experts.
  - The XPT groups were still far apart after months of paid, structured debate, and the follow-up with sceptics and concerned experts (FRI, 2023, published as Rosenberg et al. 2025, D5) moved little: skeptics' median 0.10% -> 0.12%, concerned 25% -> 20% for existential catastrophe by 2100 (Roots PDF p. 3 and 14) `[V]`. Over a thousand years both reach 30-40%.
  - Later data on "real forecasters": Metaculus Pros (professional forecasters recruited for track records on resolved questions) put human extinction from any cause before 2100 at 7.5% (31 Pros), the crowd at 2%, and the 28 forecasters who answered all five x-risk questions at 22%; Pros put 70% on AI being the cause of a global catastrophe ([Metaculus, 2026-09-23](https://metaculus.substack.com/p/will-ai-cause-human-extinction-forecasts-pro-forecasters#:~:text=the%2031%20Pros%20currently%20active%20put%20it%20at%207.5%25)) `[V]`. Metaculus itself warns the numbers depend on question wording: "fewer than half (43 of 91) keep their own 2 numbers in that order." These are not XPT superforecasters, but they are a more recent professional-forecaster number than 0.38%, and it is higher by a large factor.
  - "A tenth of Alexander's own estimate": 3% (by 2100, AI experts) versus Scott's 20-30% (undated, "maybe centuries later"): different horizons. The superforecaster number is 1/50 to 1/80 of it.
- **How checked:** downloaded the 754-page XPT PDF, pdftotext, page numbers against the PDF page index (not the printed number); read Table 9 and the group tables, Appendix 3; Metaculus post.

### D9. Narayanan and Kapoor; Nordhaus and Trembath
- **Pinker:** "As the computer scientists Arvind Narayanan and Sayash Kapoor have argued, 'AI existential risk probabilities are too unreliable to inform policy.' (Ted Nordhaus and Alex Trembath are less tactful: 'p(Doom) Is Stupid.') Numerical estimates ... are not forecasts but moods with decimal points." (L80)
- **Verdict:** ✅ Both titles are exact and the authors are right. One context point: N&K's objection is to using the numbers in public policy, and neither piece says AI risk should be dismissed.
- **Evidence `[V]`:**
  - Narayanan and Kapoor, "AI existential risk probabilities are too unreliable to inform policy", subtitle "How speculation gets laundered through pseudo-quantification", AI Snake Oil/Normal Tech, 2024-07-26. "Our central claim is that AI x-risk forecasts are far too unreliable to be useful for policy, and in fact highly misleading." ([post](https://www.normaltech.ai/p/ai-existential-risk-probabilities#:~:text=Our%20central%20claim%20is,useful%20for%20policy)). "We have no objection to AI x-risk forecasting as an academic activity, and forecasts may be helpful to companies and other private decision makers. We only question its use in the context of public policy."
  - Their three-way argument (no reference class, no theory, forecaster skill cannot be measured), and the XPT: "the most elaborate and well-executed x-risk forecasting exercise conducted to date" ([link](https://www.normaltech.ai/p/ai-existential-risk-probabilities#:~:text=the%20most%20elaborate%20and%20well%2Dexecuted%20x%2Drisk%20forecasting%20exercise)). On skill: log-score distinguishing a 1% forecaster from a 1-in-a-million forecaster needs "on the order of a hundred million" forecasts (Brier: "a trillion").
  - Nordhaus and Trembath, "p(Doom) Is Stupid: Separating AI Safety from Science Fiction", Breakthrough Journal, 2026-11-18. "But unlike other projections and probabilities, p(doom) is not really based on anything verifiable or falsifiable." ([post](https://www.breakthroughjournal.org/p/pdoom-is-stupid#:~:text=p%28doom%29%20is%20not%20really%20based%20on%20anything%20verifiable%20or%20falsifiable)). They also write that p(doom) "is of a piece with a broader tendency towards meaningless numeracy, most especially in the effective altruism community". Their section "Catastrophic, Not Existential" says "human extinction is not the standard by which we should be evaluating AI safety", i.e. they want the focus moved to catastrophic risks.
- **Context / what's left out:**
  - N&K do not say a deadline is the problem. Their issue is reference classes and verifying skill; Pinker's "no deadline" (D10) is his own add-on. The thought experiment they use shows that even dated forecasts cannot be scored for rare events, which is a stronger point than Pinker's.
  - Nordhaus and Trembath end by saying the p(doom) problem should not be an excuse to dismiss AI safety; they also dispute RAND's geoengineering route (D3). Both pieces fit Pinker's Section V "point of agreement" better than his Section IV mockery.
  - Ted Nordhaus is a coauthor of the Ecomodernist Manifesto (relevant to batch F, not here).
- **How checked:** fetched normaltech.ai post (raw HTML); Breakthrough Journal Substack copy (the thebreakthrough.org URL is Cloudflare-blocked; the Substack copy is the same essay).

### D10. "No deadline ... meaningless ... never scored"; "no base rate"
- **Pinker:** "Alexander's 'about a 25-30% chance that AI drives humanity extinct' has no deadline, so it is meaningless as a probability and can never be scored." "There is no base rate for a species being exterminated by its own technology (though there is one for prophecies that it will be)." "A p(doom) can never be scored, since if the prediction comes true there will be no one left to check it." (L66-70)
- **Verdict:** 🟡 The factual part about Scott's number is correct (no deadline). "Meaningless as a probability" does not follow, "can never be scored" is only true of the single event, and the field has many dated, resolvable doom questions, including Pinker's own example.
- **Evidence for Pinker:**
  - The quote is verbatim from Scott's open letter: "I've previously said that I think there's about a 25-30% chance that AI drives humanity extinct." (post.md L129; the link goes to "My AI Opinions", where the figure is 20%, A2). No deadline in either. In "My AI Opinions": "p(doom) calculations don't give points for delay - maybe centuries later" ([ACX](https://www.astralcodexten.com/p/my-ai-opinions)) `[V]` (sources/A-acx-my-ai-opinions-2026.txt), so Scott's own number is explicitly timeless.
  - Wikipedia's lead lists the ambiguity as a criticism: "the time frame that the probability covers" `[V]` (D7).
  - Single-event scoring: with extinction there is no later world in which a "yes" can be scored, and N&K add that a log score needs about a hundred million forecasts to tell 1% from 1 in a million (D9) `[V]`. RAND says forecasting extinction likelihood is "inappropriate as analytical tools" (D3) `[V]`. XPT itself calls extinction an "unresolvable" question and scored it by reciprocal scoring, not truth (D8) `[V]`.
  - Base rate: N&K: "For existential risk from AI, there is no reference class, as it is an event like no other." `[V]`. The concerned side agrees base rates don't help: "Base rates are not very helpful if AGI is as transformative as 15% year on year growth" (Pascal, [FRI Roots](https://forecastingresearch.org/pdf/roots-of-disagreement-on-ai-risk.pdf#page=73)) `[V]`.
  - Scott himself, in the letter, leaves the opening: "I don't claim that there is no conceivable argument for why these probabilistic estimates might be less valuable than the sorts of probabilistic estimates in a traditional superforecasting tournament. I claim that, if you had such an argument, you should have made it." (post.md L315). The deadline and base-rate points are that argument, made now. (post.md has no mention of a deadline.)
- **Evidence against / counter-arguments:**
  - Undated is not meaningless: "AI eventually causes human extinction" is a well-defined event, so a probability for it is well-defined; it is just not resolvable on a human timescale. Scott's defence in the letter is of "In Defense Of Non-Frequentist Probabilities" (a geologist's 5% eruption chance conveys information without a frequency) (post.md L317). Dated versions exist and are scorable in the world where they come out No:
    - XPT: "By 2030, 2050, 2100" for extinction, "AI-driven extinction by 2100" (D8).
    - Caplan-Yudkowsky: a dated bet, which Pinker cites (D4).
    - Metaculus: "extinction before 2100" (community 2%, Pros 7.5%, 1,580 forecasters) and a question on AI causing the human population to fall below 5,000 before 2100 (D8, [Metaculus](https://metaculus.substack.com/p/will-ai-cause-human-extinction-forecasts-pro-forecasters)) `[V]`; Anthropic's Hubinger ">10% within the next decade" (as reported there) `[V]`. The Metaculus post also shows that dating resolves the ambiguity: it warns that undated or differently dated numbers don't compare.
    - The 2023 AI Impacts survey asked about "the next 100 years" (Wikipedia lead, D7) `[V]`.
  - Scoring the doomer's forecasting skill does not need the extinction event. Tetlock's method (Scott's letter) scores a forecaster's record on resolvable questions, and the XPT/Metaculus design scores the unresolvable ones by reciprocal scoring, which "can be as accurate as forecasts incentivized using comparisons to the truth" (XPT PDF p. 9) `[V]` (N&K counter that the incentive is to predict each other, and skill cannot be checked on tail events). So the claim "never scored" is true of the extinction event only.
  - Scott's own p(doom) is not only unconditioned-and-undated: he states "p(doom|no pause)" and "p(doom|maybe-pause)" (D6), and gives dated things such as the 2100 utopia chance (40%) in the same post.
  - Base rate: "no base rate" is a contested reference-class choice, not a fact. N&K call it "a matter of degree, not kind" (reference class for geopolitical events is also contested); Tetlock treats "Grexit" as having no precedent yet forecastable via a broader comparison class (quoted by N&K) `[V]`. Candidate classes in use: natural-extinction rates (Ord), past catastrophic technologies, species-extinction rates. Pinker himself offers a base rate for failed prophecies (his own reference class, L233-241) and Tetlock's expert-judgement research; N&K say AI is "far less" analogous even so.
  - Staged-decomposition forecasting is also a base-rate-free method (D1), and the FRI sceptics themselves use it.
- **Context / what's left out:**
  - Pinker's claim is about Scott's one number. It would be accurate to call a number with no stated horizon uncomparable with a 2031 or 2100 forecast (see D2, D8), and the XPT-style numbers Pinker prefers are dated but also unscorable on the event.
  - The more damaging version for p(doom) is N&K's, not Pinker's: calibration on rare tail events cannot be learned from any feasible track record.
- **How checked:** post.md and Scott's ACX essays; Metaculus post; XPT and FRI PDFs; N&K and Breakthrough essays; Wikipedia revision text.
