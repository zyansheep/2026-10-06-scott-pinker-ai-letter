### G1. General factor, "mathematical necessity", Ruan et al. 80%
- **Pinker:** "As a mathematical necessity, any set of intercorrelated measures will yield a first principal component." (L133); and, of Scott, "a single factor explains about 80 percent of the variance in AI benchmark scores" (L131).
- **Verdict:** 🟡 The maths sentence is true but proves little. The 80% is quoted correctly from Scott, but the paper's own point is stronger than "a statistical summary".
- **Evidence:**
  - Ruan et al. do say it: "Surprisingly, we find that the first PC alone explains nearly 80% of the variation in LM capabilities." ([Ruan, Maddison, Hashimoto, arXiv v3, §3.2](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.10938#:~:text=Surprisingly%2C%20we%20find%20that,in%20LM%20capabilities)) [V]
  - Scope: 8 benchmarks (MMLU, ARC-C, HellaSwag, Winogrande, GSM8K, HumanEval, TruthfulQA, XWinograd), pretrained base models only: "we consider only pretrained base models" ([same paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.10938#:~:text=we%20consider%20only%20pretrained%20base%20models)); the 8 benchmarks are at ([same paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.10938#:~:text=we%20included%20ARC%2DC)) [V]. So Scott's "any benchmark, ranging from grammar to... software engineering" (post.md) is wider than the data. This was already rated 🟡 in the earlier report; it still holds.
  - Top 3 PCs explain "~97%"; "PC-1 represents the 'general capability' as a weighted average of all metrics" ([paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.10938#:~:text=PC%2D1%20represents%20the%20%E2%80%9Cgeneral,of%20all%20metrics)) [V].
  - Against Pinker's "just a statistical summary": the paper shows PC-1 is log-linear in training compute within model families (R² > 0.9) and uses it to predict held-out larger models (GPT-4 agent scores predicted from smaller models). That is a predictive regularity, not only a description. (Section text read in the HTML; the exact "R² > 0.9" phrase is in the intro and §3.3.) [V]
  - For Pinker: the maths. Any data matrix has a first PC; a dominant one needs positive correlations (a positive manifold). His "intercorrelated" is the right qualifier for a large PC1, but then the finding is the *size* of PC1 (80%), which he treats as trivial.
- **Newer (2026):** The general factor is contested, not gone.
  - Krakauer: on 39 models x 14 benchmarks, all 28 pairwise correlations positive across 8 benchmarks; "PC1 explains 90% of variance on a 5-benchmark core battery... reducing to 77% by 2024"; on a four-benchmark battery PC1 peaks at 92% and drops to 64% "with the arrival of reasoning-specialized models in 2024" ([arXiv 2604.09911, 2026-04-10](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.09911)) [V, abstract].
  - Haznitrama et al., 1,618 models x 456 benchmarks: a general factor "accounts for 70.8% of variance in model performance at our most generous estimate, and far less than that in most of our solutions"; "Our findings go against current endeavors of defining, identifying, and targeting general intelligence as a tangible construct in language model development" ([arXiv 2609.36515, 2026-09-29](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.36515)) [V, abstract].
  - Read together: a large first factor exists and is stable at 65-90% on small curated sets, but it falls on bigger, sparser, newer sets and as reasoning models arrive. That helps Pinker's "jagged" point more than the paper he is answering does. It also shows he did not cite any of it.
- **Context / what's left out:** Pinker never says the 80% is of a model-by-benchmark matrix of open base models from 2023-24. He is not wrong about it; Scott's wording is the loose one.
- **How checked:** Fetched arXiv HTML of 2405.10938 (v3), extracted text; arXiv abs pages for 2604.09911, 2609.36515; web search for discovery.

### G2. Chimpanzee outperforms students; pigeons and Monty Hall
- **Pinker:** "In the lab, a young chimpanzee outperformed university students at recalling the positions of numerals flashed on a screen, and pigeons learn to switch doors in the Monty Hall problem more reliably than people." (L137)
- **Verdict:** 🟡 Both sentences report what the original papers found. For the chimp, two published critiques showing the comparison was confounded by practice go unmentioned. For the pigeons, replications are mixed.
- **Evidence (chimp):**
  - Original: Inoue & Matsuzawa 2007, [Working memory of numerals in chimpanzees](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2007.10.027) (Current Biology; I could not get an abstract from Crossref or Europe PMC, so I rely on the critiques' descriptions of it [2nd]).
  - Silberberg & Kearns 2009: "Ayumu had many sessions of practice on their task before terminal performances were measured; their human subjects had none. The present report shows that when two humans are given practice in the Inoue and Matsuzawa (2007) memory task, their accuracy levels match those of Ayumu." ([Animal Cognition, doi 10.1007/s10071-008-0206-8](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10071-008-0206-8), abstract via Europe PMC) [V]
  - Cook & Wilson 2010: "the chimpanzee received extensive practice on a non-time-pressured version of the task; the human subjects received none. Here we report that, after adequate practice, 2 university students substantially outperformed the chimpanzee. There is no evidence for a superior or qualitatively different spatial memory system in chimpanzees." ([Psychonomic Bulletin & Review, doi 10.3758/pbr.17.4.599](https://doi.org/10.3758/pbr.17.4.599), abstract via Europe PMC) [V]
  - The critiques say the comparison group was 9 (Silberberg) or 3 (Cook & Wilson) students against one chimpanzee at the headline number. Pinker's "outperformed university students" is the original claim; the two critiques are about the conclusion he draws (jagged profiles). His point (some tasks, animals do well) survives; this specific example does not show a superior memory.
- **Evidence (pigeons):**
  - Original: "birds adjusted their probability of switching and staying to approximate the optimal strategy... Replication of the procedure with human participants showed that humans failed to adopt optimal strategies, even with extensive training." ([Herbranson & Schroeder 2010, doi 10.1037/a0017703](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0017703), abstract via Europe PMC) [V]
  - Replication, mixed: "The college students began to switch on a majority of trials much sooner than the pigeons, contrary to the findings by Herbranson and Schroeder (2010) that pigeons perform better than people on this task." ([Mazur & Kahlbaugh 2012, doi 10.1037/a0028273](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0028273)) [V]
  - "We found that pigeons performed only slightly better than humans" ([Stagner et al., Behav. Processes 2015, doi 10.1016/j.beproc.2014.10.008](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.beproc.2014.10.008), abstract) [V]
  - Pinker's "more reliably than people" is the 2010 result; later labs found a much smaller or no pigeon advantage.
- **How checked:** Crossref (titles/dates), Europe PMC abstracts by DOI; web discovery for the replications.

### G3. Number systems, natural frequencies, analog medium
- **Pinker:** "two mental systems for number: an exact one that tracks individual objects up to about three or four, and an approximate one that estimates larger magnitudes with an error proportional to their size"; "People neglect base rates when a problem is posed in terms of single-event probabilities, but often reason like good Bayesians when the same information is presented as natural frequencies"; "People solve many spatial problems (and indeed many abstract conceptual problems) in an analog, graphical medium." (L139)
- **Verdict:** 🟡 The first two are standard and the DOIs resolve to the right papers. The third cites a book that argues one side of a live debate, and the "abstract conceptual problems" extension is not what that book is about.
- **Evidence:**
  - DOIs check: 10.1016/j.tics.2004.05.002 is Feigenson, Dehaene & Spelke, "Core systems of number" (2004); 10.1037/0033-295x.102.4.684 is Gigerenzer & Hoffrage, "How to improve Bayesian reasoning without instruction: Frequency formats" (1995); 10.1016/0010-0277(95)00664-8 is Cosmides & Tooby, "Are humans good intuitive statisticians after all?" (1996) [V, Crossref]. Note: Pinker links the *natural frequencies* anchor text to Cosmides & Tooby, and *good Bayesians* to Gigerenzer & Hoffrage; both are fine.
  - The Google Books id `igi-Z_w38CUC` is Kosslyn, Thompson & Ganis, *The Case for Mental Imagery* (OUP, 2006) [V, [books page](https://books.google.com/books?id=igi-Z_w38CUC)]. It argues that mental images are depictive (analog). That is contested (the propositional side, Pylyshyn). I did not look for the counter-literature in detail.
- **Context / what's left out:** The natural-frequency effect is real but not universal: "often" is fair. I did not check the Barbey & Sloman nested-sets critique against his wording.
- **How checked:** Crossref for the three DOIs; Google Books page for the book id.

### G4. "Vision language models are blind"
- **Pinker:** "multimodal models that ace standard vision benchmarks were only 58 percent accurate on average at simple tasks like judging whether two circles overlap or counting how many times two lines cross, 'far from the human expected accuracy of 100%.'" (L139)
- **Verdict:** 🟡 Quote and number are verbatim from the paper. But (1) the 58% is a mean over four mid-2024 models and seven tasks, not a score on the two tasks named; (2) 100% for humans is "expected", not measured; (3) the paper is 2.5 years old and current models score far higher on the same benchmark.
- **Evidence:**
  - "four state-of-the-art VLMs are only 58.07% accurate on average. Sonnet-3.5 performs the best at 77.84% accuracy, far from the human expected accuracy of 100%." ([arXiv 2407.06581v6 abstract, 2025-03-27](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.06581#:~:text=four%20state%2Dof%2Dthe%2Dart%20VLMs%20are%20only%2058.07%25%20accurate%20on%20average)) [V]
  - Models: GPT-4o, Gemini-1.5 Pro, Claude 3 Sonnet, Claude 3.5 Sonnet. Table 1: the per-model 7-task means are 50.23, 58.48, 45.73 and 77.84; mean 58.07 ([PDF p.4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.06581#page=4)) [V].
  - On the two tasks Pinker names, the four-model means are 56.84 (line intersections, Table 1a) and 86.70 (two circles overlap, Table 1b: "accuracy is 86.70%", [PDF p.9](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.06581#page=9)) [V, task mapping from the paper's text]. So "58 percent ... at ... judging whether two circles overlap" overstates the circles result by about 29 points.
  - Same paper: "VLMs including slow-thinking models consistently struggle" (Gemini 2.0 Flash-Thinking and QVQ tested, no better) [V].
  - **Current:** Qwen's Qwen3.5 model card (self-reported) lists "VlmsAreBlind" for GPT-5-mini 75.8, Claude Sonnet 4.5 85.5, Qwen3-VL-235B 79.5, Qwen3.5-122B-A10B 96.7, Qwen3.5-27B 96.9, Qwen3.5-35B-A3B 97.0 ([model card](https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3.5-35B-A3B#:~:text=VlmsAreBlind,97.0)) [V]. The llm-stats leaderboard (self-reported, 6 models, "last updated November 24, 2026") has Qwen3.5-35B-A3B and Qwen3.6-27B at 0.970 and Seed 1.8 at 0.930 ([page](https://llm-stats.com/benchmarks/vlmsareblind#:~:text=Qwen3.5%2D35B%2DA3B,0.970)) [V].
- **Context / what's left out:**
  - These are vendor-reported on a possibly different subset and scoring harness, and the leaderboard has no verified results. A third-party aggregator snippet I saw had GPT-5.2 High 84.2 and Claude Opus 4.5 77.2 [2nd, not fetched]. So the picture is "gap largely closed for the best 2026 vision models, not for all".
  - The paper itself shows the failure is in decoding ("vision encoders contain sufficient visual information"), which is not a deep limit.
  - Pinker's own tense "were only 58 percent" is correct, but the sentence sits in an argument about what AI "lacks" (L139: "which lacks such a medium").
- **How checked:** Fetched arXiv PDF v6, read Table 1 and text; fetched the Qwen model card and llm-stats page; web search for third-party numbers.

### G5. Where the training steps came from: InstructGPT, Llama 2, Scale, Sutskever
- **Pinker:** "Supervised Fine Tuning (providing a model with tens of thousands of possible instructions and their ideal answers) and Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (hiring tens of thousands of contractors to rank output answers)... (The paper introducing the technique began, 'Making language models bigger does not inherently make them better at following a user's intent.')... began to show diminishing returns. Ilya Sutskever... said in 2024 that 'Pre-training as we know it will unquestionably end,' because 'There's only one internet.'" (L143)
- **Verdict:** 🟡 Every quote is verbatim. The "tens of thousands of contractors" claim is loosely supported by a trade-press line about a different activity, and "diminishing returns" is Pinker's gloss, not Sutskever's claim.
- **Evidence:**
  - InstructGPT opening (the abstract's first sentence): "Making language models bigger does not inherently make them better at following a user's intent." ([arXiv 2203.02155](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.02155#:~:text=Making%20language%20models%20bigger,following%20a%20user)) [V]. It is the *abstract's* opening, and that paper "introduces" RLHF for instruction following, not RLHF generally (the technique predates it). Pinker says "the technique" for SFT+RLHF together, which is what the paper does.
  - InstructGPT's own scale is small: "we first hire a team of 40 contractors to label"; SFT set "about 13k training prompts", RM 33k, PPO 31k ([PDF p.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2203.02155#page=2), [p.7](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2203.02155#page=7)) [V]. So the "tens of thousands" of anything in the original paper is prompts, not contractors.
  - Llama 2: "We found that SFT annotations in the order of tens of thousands was enough to achieve a high-quality result. We stopped annotating SFT after collecting a total of 27,540 annotations." ([arXiv 2307.09288 PDF p.9](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2307.09288#page=9)) [V]. Pinker's "tens of thousands of possible instructions" matches. Left out: the same section says "we focused first on collecting several thousand examples" and that fewer, higher-quality examples beat "millions of examples from third-party datasets", i.e. it is a quality-over-quantity result.
  - Scale: "Outlier... paid out hundreds of millions of dollars to tens of thousands of freelance contributors around the world over roughly the past year" ([Fast Company, 2024-11-25, Wayback copy](https://web.archive.org/web/20260526213231/https://www.fastcompany.com/91234864/how-scale-became-the-go-to-company-for-ai-training#:~:text=paid%20out%20hundreds%20of%20millions%20of%20dollars%20to%20tens,of%20freelance%20contributors)) [V]. These are "freelance contributors" on a platform that also does coding, music, law and non-RLHF work, not a count of people "to rank output answers". Fast Company says Scale started RLHF work with OpenAI "about three years ago".
  - Sutskever, NeurIPS 2024 test-of-time talk, auto-captions of the YouTube video (I downloaded them): "pre-training as we know it will unquestionably end"; "The data is not growing because we have but one internet... data is the fossil fuel of AI... we've achieved peak data, and there'll be no more... Now, it's still... let us go quite far... but this is there's only one internet." (youtube `1yvBqasHLZs`, `sources/G-sutskever-neurips2024-autosubs.vtt`) [V, auto-captions]. So both "we have but one internet" and "there's only one internet" were said; Pinker's wording is what the Verge printed ([Verge](https://www.theverge.com/2024/12/13/24320811/what-ilya-sutskever-sees-openai-model-data-training#:~:text=There%E2%80%99s%20only%20one%20internet)) [V]. The "unquestionably" is verbatim ([Verge](https://www.theverge.com/2024/12/13/24320811/what-ilya-sutskever-sees-openai-model-data-training#:~:text=Pre%2Dtraining%20as%20we%20know%20it%20will%20unquestionably%20end)) [V].
  - "Diminishing returns": Sutskever's 2024 claim is a *data* limit ("peak data"), not a statement about returns. In Nov 2025 he says "At some point though, pre-training will run out of data. The data is very clearly finite." and also "it appears that Gemini have found a way to get more out of pre-training" ([Dwarkesh, 2025-11-25](https://www.dwarkesh.com/p/ilya-sutskever-2#:~:text=At%20some%20point%20though%2C%20pre%2Dtraining%20will%20run%20out%20of%20data)) [V]. He frames the shift as "we are back to the age of research" ([same](https://www.dwarkesh.com/p/ilya-sutskever-2#:~:text=But%20now%20that%20compute,age%20of%20research)) [V]. A search snippet reports he later wrote on X that "Scaling the current thing will keep leading to improvements" [2nd, not verified; I did not pull the tweet].
- **Context / what's left out:** "Even the ingredient that most resembles a one-dimensional conception of intelligence... began to show diminishing returns" is a reasonable reading of "age of research", but nobody in the sources calls it diminishing returns; the same sources say scale still helps.
- **How checked:** arXiv PDFs; Wayback copy of Fast Company (original is JS-gated); Verge HTML; `yt-dlp` auto-captions; Dwarkesh transcript.

### G6. "Vintage" and "time-capsule" LLMs
- **Pinker:** "Could a model trained in 1900 reason intelligently about genetic engineering, quantum computing, nuclear power, streaming comedies... Studies of 'vintage' and 'time-capsule' LLMs suggest not." (L145)
- **Verdict:** 🟡 Neither source tests that question. They show that such models lack post-cutoff facts (trivial) and, for the one real study, report partial generalization beyond its data.
- **Evidence:**
  - Gigazine (2026-04-28) summarising talkie-1930 (13B, pre-1930 text): detailed answers for pre-1930 questions; "information about 1930 and later, such as 'the conflict between communism and capitalism led to the Cold War,' was not included"; it measured "unexpectedness" of ~5,000 NYT "On This Day" events, which "increased for events occurring after the knowledge cutoff"; it wrote simple Python though "its performance was significantly inferior to models trained on modern web data" and "it was able to derive the correct answer by simply replacing addition with subtraction, suggesting that it may have some understanding of the concept of 'inverse functions'" ([Gigazine](https://gigazine.net/gsc_news/en/20260428-talkie-vintage-language-model#:~:text=it%20was%20able%20to,addition%20with%20subtraction)) [V].
  - The primary page: vintage models "dramatically underperform models trained on web data (which includes code), we've found that they are slowly but steadily improving at this task with scale" ([talkie-lm.com](https://talkie-lm.com/introducing-talkie#:~:text=we%E2%80%99ve%20found%20that%20they,task%20with%20scale)) [V]. It lists "could a model trained up to 1911 independently discover General Relativity" as an open question, not a result. Early versions also leaked post-1930 facts (WWII, New Deal) [V, same page].
  - PopSci (2026-01-24) on TimeCapsuleLLM, a student's hobby model on 1800-1875 London text: "This is, to be clear, very much a hobby project. The sample-generated text on GitHub isn't consistently coherent." It discusses a PNAS opinion piece on using such models to study past psychology. Nothing about reasoning on later topics ([PopSci](https://www.popsci.com/technology/this-ai-thinks-its-the-1800s/#:~:text=very%20much%20a%20hobby%20project)) [V].
- **Context / what's left out:** Pinker's conclusion (no knowledge of later things, so no ability to reason about them) is plausible, but "suggest not" overstates two pieces that are about something else: data quality, OCR and leakage (Gigazine/talkie) and a psychology-research proposal (PopSci). The talkie result that surprise rises after the cutoff is the most relevant data point, and it supports "doesn't know the future", not "can't reason about it".
- **How checked:** Fetched both pages and the talkie primary post.

### G7. Hallucination: Xu et al. 2024 and OpenAI (Kalai et al. 2025)
- **Pinker:** "A formal analysis from 2024 concludes that 'it is impossible to eliminate hallucination in LLMs.' OpenAI's own researchers explain that 'if incorrect statements cannot be distinguished from facts, then hallucinations in pretrained language models will arise through natural statistical pressures.'" (L147)
- **Verdict:** 🟡 Both quotes are verbatim. But the OpenAI paper explicitly cites Xu et al. as a view it disputes, and its main proposal is a fix. Pinker uses them as if they agreed.
- **Evidence:**
  - Xu, Jain, Kankanhalli abstract: "we formalize the problem and show that it is impossible to eliminate hallucination in LLMs." ([arXiv 2401.11817v2, PDF p.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2401.11817#page=1)) [V]. Their result is for any *computable* LLM used as a general problem solver in a formal world; they say real-world models are included "Since the formal world is a part of the real world". They also discuss mitigators.
  - Kalai, Nachum, Vempala, Zhang abstract, quote verbatim as Pinker gives it ([arXiv 2509.04664, PDF p.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.04664#page=1)) [V]. The same abstract continues: "We then argue that hallucinations persist due to the way most evaluations are graded... This 'epidemic' of penalizing uncertain responses can only be addressed through a socio-technical mitigation: modifying the scoring of existing benchmarks."
  - The paper rebuts the Xu claim by name: "Hallucinations are inevitable only for base models. Many have argued that hallucinations are inevitable (Jones, 2025; Leffer, 2024; Xu et al., 2024). However, a non-hallucinating model could be easily created, using a question-answer database and a calculator... and otherwise outputs IDK." ([PDF p.8](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.04664#page=8)) [V]. The OpenAI summary post: "Claim: Hallucinations are inevitable. Finding: They are not, because language models can abstain when uncertain." ([OpenAI](https://www.openai.com/index/why-language-models-hallucinate/#:~:text=Claim%3A%20Hallucinations%20are%20inevitable.,abstain%20when%20uncertain)) [V]. Same post: "Accuracy will never reach 100%".
  - It also says post-training reduces them ("RLHF... DPO... have been shown to reduce hallucinations", p.5ff) and that "search and reasoning" are "not panaceas" ([p.15](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.04664#page=15)) [V].
- **Context / what's left out:** Pinker's sentence is right that pretrained models will make errors where facts are unlearnable (e.g. random birthdays). What he omits is that OpenAI draws the opposite conclusion from Xu on "impossible to eliminate" and proposes changing benchmark scoring so models abstain. He also places "formal analysis" next to "OpenAI's own researchers" as two voices for one thesis; they are not.
- **Newer (2025-26):** Artificial Analysis's AA-Omniscience (6,000 questions; index penalises wrong answers and rewards abstaining) found at launch "Claude 4.1 Opus attains the highest score (4.8), making it one of only three models to score above zero. These results reveal persistent factuality and calibration weaknesses across frontier models" ([arXiv 2511.13029, 2025-11-17](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.13029)) [V, abstract]. Aggregator pages claim large 2026 drops in hallucination rate through abstention; I could not verify those numbers from a primary leaderboard, so I do not rely on them [2nd].
- **How checked:** Read both PDFs; fetched the OpenAI post; arXiv abstract for AA-Omniscience.

### G8. Generalisation failures: reversal curse, GSM-Symbolic, counterfactual analogies, CoT mirage
- **Pinker:** "An LLM trained that A is B does not automatically infer that B is A. Changing nothing but the numbers in grade-school arithmetic problems lowered the scores of every model tested, and models that solved published analogy problems faltered on 'counterfactual' variants that people found no harder. A controlled study of chain-of-thought reasoning concluded that it 'is a brittle mirage when it is pushed beyond training distributions.' These failures are becoming rarer with each generation..." (L149-151)
- **Verdict:** 🟡 for the group. Reversal curse ✅ as stated (dated models), GSM-Symbolic 🟡 ("nothing but the numbers", "every model" both wrong as stated), analogies 🟡 (GPT-3/3.5/4 only, plus a published rebuttal), CoT mirage 🟡 (verbatim, but tiny from-scratch models). His own last sentence concedes the main point about age.
- **Reversal curse (2309.12288):**
  - "GPT-4 correctly answers questions like the former 79% of the time, compared to 33% for the latter." ([PDF p.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2309.12288#page=1)) [V]. The claim is for models *finetuned* on fictitious facts; the paper says "The Reversal Curse does not apply for in-context learning" ([p.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2309.12288#page=2)) [V]. Pinker's "does not automatically infer" is accurate.
  - Newer: I found no 2025-26 test of frontier chat models on the celebrity-parent or fictitious-fact setup. What exists: "Improving Latent Generalization Using Test-time Compute" (2026-04): RL-trained long CoT "resolves many instances of latent generalization failures", though "on pure reversal tasks, we find that thinking does not unlock direct knowledge inversion... thinking models still remain well below the performance of in-context learning" ([arXiv 2604.01430](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.01430)) [V, abstract]; and LLaDA (diffusion model) "addresses the reversal curse, surpassing GPT-4o in a reversal poem completion task" ([arXiv 2502.09992](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.09992)) [V, abstract, authors' claim]. So the limit persists in the weights of standard autoregressive models, with workarounds.
- **GSM-Symbolic (2410.05229):**
  - The paper's abstract says: "the performance of models declines when only the numerical values in the question are altered" and "the performance of all models drops on GSM-Symbolic (Sec. 4.1)" ([PDF p.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.05229#page=1)) [V]. Pinker follows the paper's summary.
  - But GSM-Symbolic changes names *and* numbers. The numbers-only comparison is shown only for a few selected models (Fig. 4, [p.7](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.05229#page=7)): "To conserve space, we present results for a few selected models in each experiment, but the full results for all models are available in Tab. 1" [V].
  - The full table does not show every model dropping. GSM8K(100) vs GSM-Symbolic: o1-mini 93.0 -> 94.5; Llama3-8b-instruct 74.0 -> 74.6; GPT-4o 95.0 -> 94.9 (paper's Fig. 2 p = 0.6, no significant change); o1-preview 96.0 -> 92.7. Against full GSM8K: GPT-4o 95.2 -> 94.9, o1-mini 95.1 -> 94.5 ([Table 1, PDF p.17](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.05229#page=17)) [V]. So "every model" holds only against the full-set baseline, and for the strongest models by under a point.
  - The big drops are for harder variants: GSM-NoOp, "catastrophic performance decline across all tested models, with the Phi-3-mini model experiencing over a 65% drop, and even stronger models such as o1-preview showing significant declines" (o1-preview 96.0 -> 77.4 in the table) [V].
  - Newer: GSM-SEM (2026-05) evaluates 14 models including o3, GPT-5 and 5.1, Gemini 2.5 Pro: average accuracy drop vs GSM8K is -2.36% on GSM-Symbolic (o3 -2.18, GPT-5 medium -1.28, Gemini 2.5 Pro -0.86), larger on their own semantic variants (-8.28% avg on GSM-Symbolic-SEM; -13.53% on GSM-Plus-SEM) ([arXiv 2605.07053](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.07053), Table 1 in [HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.07053)) [V]. So the sensitivity persists but is small for number swaps and bigger for changes that alter the underlying facts.
- **Counterfactual analogies (2402.08955):**
  - Lewis & Mitchell tested GPT-3, GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 (turbo-0613) on letter-string analogies with permuted or symbol alphabets: "the GPT models' performance declines sharply on the counterfactual set" while "human performance... stays relatively constant across alphabets" ([arXiv 2402.08955](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.08955)) [V]. "that people found no harder": supported, the paper says human performance stays high.
  - Rebuttal Pinker does not mention: Webb, Holyoak & Lu argue the permuted-alphabet tasks need exact counting, and that GPT-4 reaches roughly human level when it can count with code execution ([arXiv 2404.13070](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2404.13070#page=2), later PNAS Nexus 2025). In the paper text I read: "GPT-4 + code execution performed on par with human participants (main effect of human participants vs. GPT-4 + code execution: P = 0.496)" while plain GPT-4 was significantly worse than humans [V]; the quoted sentence above is my paraphrase of that. This is a small rebuttal-paper test on GPT-4, not a frontier-model result.
  - Lewis & Mitchell's own follow-up (arXiv 2411.14215, Nov 2024) concludes the models "often lack the robustness of zero-shot human analogy-making" but says it did not test "models specifically trained for reasoning" [V, line 85 of PDF text]. I found no 2025-26 paper that runs reasoning models on the counterfactual letter-strings (arXiv listing query was rate-limited partway; this is a gap, not evidence of absence).
- **CoT mirage (2508.01191):**
  - "we reveal that CoT reasoning is a brittle mirage when it is pushed beyond training distributions" ([PDF p.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.01191#page=1), v6, 2026-05-08) [V]. Verbatim.
  - What the "controlled study" is: models "with GPT and LLaMA architectures and parameter sizes ranging from 62K to 3B when training from scratch" on a synthetic task (DataAlchemy: ciphers and shifts on letter strings) ([p.6](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.01191#page=6)) [V]. Only two real models, LLaMA3-8B and Qwen3-14B-Instruct, were fine-tuned on that synthetic task. The authors' limitation: "Our controlled experiments are conducted on the abstract environment DataAlchemy... may inevitably not fully capture the semantic richness... of natural language" ([p.23](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.01191#page=23)) [V]. No frontier reasoning model was tested.
  - Newer: I found no 2026 replication on frontier models; critiques were blog/HN-level (toy-scale models) [2nd].
- **Context / what's left out:** Pinker's last sentence ("These failures are becoming rarer with each generation") is fair and is supported by the 2026 GSM-SEM and vision-leaderboard numbers above. But the paragraph's evidence is all from 2023-24 models (GPT-3 to GPT-4, Llama-3-8B, Gemma-2, o1-preview) except the CoT paper, which uses toy models. He presents no 2025-26 frontier test of any of the four.
